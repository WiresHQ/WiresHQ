US inflation hit 3.7% in September, hotter than expected, and Fed rate hike odds for 28 October jumped to about 44%. Here is what happened and what is next.

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics said the Consumer Price Index rose 0.6% in September from August and 3.7% from a year earlier. That is up from 3.4% in August and slightly above the 3.6% economists expected. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year, a touch hotter than forecast.

Energy did most of the damage. Gasoline prices jumped 9.1% in September and accounted for more than half of the monthly rise, with several outlets linking the surge to the war involving Iran. AAA puts the national average at $4.33 a gallon, about 3.27 pounds, compared with $2.98 before the war began. Food prices were flat on the month and up 2.5% over the year.

The Federal Reserve, led by Chair Kevin Warsh, raised rates on 16 September for the first time since 2023, to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%. After the inflation report, market-implied odds of another hike at the 28 October meeting rose from about 22% the day before to about 44%, according to CME FedWatch. Those odds are market expectations, not official forecasts, and nothing has been decided. Treasury yields rose and gold fell in early trading.

In the UK, the same global moves matter through sterling and the mortgage and savings rates that follow Bank Rate and market rates. UK inflation was 3.1% in August and Bank Rate stands at 3.75%. The Office for National Statistics publishes September UK inflation on 21 October, and Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey and the Monetary Policy Committee decide on Bank Rate on 5 November. US and UK figures were measured at different dates, so they should not be compared directly.

The key dates ahead are 20 October for UK jobs data, 21 October for UK CPI, 28 October for the Fed decision and 5 November for the Bank of England.
