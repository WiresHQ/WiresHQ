Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor's search warrants were unlawful, the High Court heard, but police say the investigation into the former Prince Andrew continues.

On Thursday 8 October 2026, the High Court in London heard that Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor and Thames Valley Police agreed the search warrants used to raid two of his properties in Norfolk and Berkshire in February were unlawful. Mr Justice Hilliard said significant errors were made, including that the warrants were granted without considering whether the items seized could include legally privileged or other protected material. Thames Valley Police said the original court's decision to issue the warrants under the specific law used was incorrect, so the warrants had to be legally quashed. The quashing was formalised on 22 July, but the case only became public this week.

The judge was clear that the ruling is not the end of the story. He said quashing the warrants does not bring the investigation to an end or mean that the whole investigation was unlawful. Police said the agreement concerns the legal basis for the warrants and is not a judgment on their ongoing investigation. Separately, police have applied to keep the material that was seized, and the court must still decide whether that is allowed. Reports say the issue will not be settled until next year.

Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor, formerly known as Prince Andrew, was arrested on 19 February 2026 on suspicion of misconduct in public office, an offence involving the abuse of a public role. After about 11 hours he was released under investigation, which means he has been neither charged nor cleared. He denies wrongdoing. The Crown Prosecution Service, the body that decides whether to bring charges, has announced no charging decision.

For American readers, two points help. In England and Wales, being released under investigation is not a clearance, and courts do not automatically exclude evidence from an unlawful search the way US courts often do. The quashed warrants therefore do not mean that any evidence is unusable, and the court has not said that.

In a separate development on Friday 9 October, the Crown Estate confirmed 302,000 pounds in compensation for the surrender of the Royal Lodge lease and said its focus is now the future use of the property.
